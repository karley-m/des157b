Project Overview

The main idea: This project combines speculative thinking, some data visualization, and utopian narratives to explore how housing might evolve in response to climate change and post-fossil-fuel living by reimagining the historic Sears house kit as a participatory, modular, climate-resilient system for future communities. It asks the question: what will housing need to look like in the future as energy infrastructure changes and climate change intesifies? Read the official proposal here.

The project's goal: Future housing should not be treated as a static product, but as an adaptable system shaped by ecology, infrastructure, community, and participation. By displaying everyone's creations together, this project aims to foster a mindset of community.

The target audience: Young adults and emerging adults navigating housing uncertainty, those frustrated by unaffordable, inflexible, developer-driven housing, and anyone interested in imagining more resilient, climate-aware alternatives.

Project Research & Brainstorming

Landing on my topic: How did I end up choosing to focus on housing specifically? Coming into this class after working on a project about Village Homes, a neighborhood in Davis, California renowned for its sustainability features and use of shared spaces, I was inspired to use speculative design to consider future possibilities of homes.

Initially, I was drawn to the idea of simulating a dystopian future, where suburban sprawl created endless car-dependent roads, surveillance systems multipled, and your landlord was AI, all displayed through a Zillow interface in 2060. However, I noticed that when planning out this project, I wanted it to end on a good note, but didn't know how to make that transition. It left me unsatisfied, and so I pivoted.

My brainstorming led me to learning about the early 1900s Sears catalog home kits which I became drawn to because of how housing was imagined as something more flexible and customizable. My goal became to allow users to customize a home and address housing needs by specific climates to promote resilience and living with one's environment. This developed into a "drag-and-drop" game where users could assemble the pieces to their liking and save the design.

I began researching the kinds of alternative energy, water, and food systems, as well as sustainable materials used in homes. I was inspired by Solarpunk speculative ways of thinking and the artistic renderings that have been created by people in that community.

In my comparitive analysis, I looked at other customizable, "drag-and-drop" games, like Neal.fun and Magic Theatre. Less specific examples, but something I thought about often, was old PBS and Sprout flash games I played as a kid that featured moveable objects as well as a combination of realistic and drawn visual elements, all in 2D.

Visual Design

To plan out all of the visuals I would need, I created a user flow chart beginning with the three climate options (dry/arid, temperate, and tropical) and listing all of the variables I would need based off of the user's choice in climate and house shape. This resulted in me producing 48 different images for each climate, house shape, and material option in Adobe Illustrator. The rest of the steps are hand-drawn in Procreate.

The rest of the interface I kept very simple, working with two fonts and keeping the background a plain white with black buttons. I didn't want to overload the project with too much color as I thought it would take away from the options being presented. I used a mixed-media collage style because it represents community as something assembled from many different parts. The layering of texutres, images, and forms mirror how people from diverse backgrounds come together to create something shared.

User Interface

The user interface follows 7 steps. The project begins with an introductory page where users scroll through some information that introduces the topic of housing. After the user has chosen an option for each of the 7 steps, they are greeted by the assembly page where they are prompted to assemble their house parts. They also have the option to add additional elements to their home like furniture, pets, a bike, and a tree that is customized to their selected climate. After saving their design, they have the option to save a PNG of their creation. Anything within the canvas is automatically uploaded to Back4App to later display in the "neighborhood."

I created a JSON file to hold all of the descriptions and compatibility scores with each option in each step. I wanted to include this kind of data visualization to help strengthen the idea of living with one's environment and how we can maximize things like water systems, the sun, and the wind to be as least destructive as we can.

The interface then became presented as followed:

  1. Choosing the parts
  2. Assembling the home
  3. Sharing with others and viewing other creations

Revisions

After conducting usability tests, I made revisions to strengthen the usability and visuals. 3 out of 3 users were unsure what the compatibility dots on the hover state meant so I created a help overlay marked by a question mark in the corner to provide more information. I also added a progress bar at the top to let the user know how much of the choosing is left to go. I noticed users were leaving their items in the canvas small, so to encourage them to make the house fill up majority of the canvas, I created a temporary frame to help guide how large the house should be.

Other small things I added was a scroll icon for the first page to prompt the user to scroll and a footer collage based on the user's climate choice in case they forget which one they chose. For the assemble page, I customized three tree options, rearranged the information about the canvas to the side to minimize the need to scroll, and added the ability to delete any of the additional images within the canvas.

Summary

Overall, getting to create this project has been a fulfilling process. By narrowing down my interest in housing to be about resilience and healthier living, I was able to research the different broad ideas surrounding speculative housing futures and implement data visualization.

By approaching the topic with an optimistic view, instead of my initial dystopian view, I felt excited to have users create their own homes in this participatory, hands-on model.

I hope this project encourages people to think differently about the built environment and question the housing models that currently shape our lives. Homes can take many forms, but what matters most is the community around them, which can become the foundation for meaningful change.
